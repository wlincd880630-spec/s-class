Choking is the phenomenon where a person with extensive practice fails at a crucial moment. It's common in high-pressure situations like sports, but also affects public speakers, contest-goers, and even renowned musicians. While many blame 'nerves,' the underlying issue, according to two sets of theories, boils down to a failure of focus.

The first set, known as 'distraction theories,' suggests that when the mind is preoccupied with worries and fears, these irrelevant thoughts compete for limited attention with the task at hand. Tasks that challenge working memory, like complex math problems, are especially vulnerable. The second set, 'explicit monitoring theories,' posits that pressure can cause people to overanalyze skills that have become automatic. Thinking consciously about the precise mechanics of a subconscious action, like a golf swing, interferes with the ability to perform it smoothly.

Research suggests individuals who are self-conscious and anxious are more susceptible to choking. However, it's not inevitable. Several strategies can help prevent it. First, practicing under stressful conditions can accustom performers to the pressure. Second, adopting a pre-performance routine, such as deep breaths or repeating a cue word, can lead to more consistent performance. Finally, maintaining an external focus on the ultimate goal (like the ball's trajectory) is more effective than an internal focus on the mechanics of one's own body movements.
