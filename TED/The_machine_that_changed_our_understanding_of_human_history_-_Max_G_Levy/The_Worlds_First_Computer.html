In 1900, divers exploring a 2,000-year-old shipwreck off the coast of Greece discovered a remarkable device. What looked like corroded metal and wood turned out to be the Antikythera mechanism, an intricate system of gears far more advanced than anything thought to exist in the ancient world. This device is now considered the world's first analog computer. It was a sophisticated astronomical calculator, capable of predicting the movements of the sun, moon, and the five planets known at the time. It could forecast eclipses, track the phase of the moon on a spinning sphere, and even indicate the timing of ancient Greek festivals. The mechanism's precision was astonishing; its gears could replicate the complex orbit of the moon with incredible accuracy, demonstrating a level of mechanical engineering that would not be seen again for over a thousand years.

The Antikythera mechanism operates on analog principles, which are very different from the digital computers we use today, like smartphones. Digital systems store information as a series of discrete numbers—zeros and ones (binary code). In contrast, analog computers use continuous physical quantities, such as mechanical motion, to directly represent information. A vinyl record is a good example, where the physical grooves are a direct impression of sound waves. This direct representation makes analog computing incredibly efficient for certain tasks. An analog scale, for instance, converts the continuous force of your weight directly into a number on a dial. The elegance and efficiency of this ancient technology are now inspiring a new wave of innovation, as researchers explore how analog principles can be integrated with modern systems to build the powerful computers of the future.
