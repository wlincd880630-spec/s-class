Even highly accomplished individuals like author Maya Angelou and physicist Albert Einstein have reported feeling like frauds, unable to internalize their success. This nagging doubt is known as imposter syndrome, an extremely common psychological pattern where people doubt their skills, talents, or accomplishments and have a persistent fear of being exposed as a 'fraud'. First studied by psychologist Pauline Rose Clance, she observed that many high-achieving students felt they didn't deserve their place at university, sometimes believing their acceptance was an error.

Imposter syndrome is not a disease or abnormality but a universal experience that can affect anyone, regardless of gender, race, age, or occupation. It often stems from specific psychological phenomena. One is the tendency for highly skilled people to assume others are just as skilled, leading them to feel they don't deserve special accolades. Another cause is 'pluralistic ignorance,' where individuals privately doubt themselves but believe they are alone in their insecurity because no one else voices similar concerns. This creates a cycle of self-doubt, as it's difficult to gauge how hard peers are working or how much they struggle.

These intense feelings of inadequacy can prevent people from sharing innovative ideas or applying for opportunities where they could excel. The most effective way to combat imposter syndrome is to talk about it. Hearing that respected mentors, advisors, or peers have also experienced these feelings can provide immense relief and normalization. Other practical strategies include consciously collecting and revisiting positive feedback to build an objective view of your competence. By fostering open conversations about professional challenges and acknowledging the simple truth—that you are talented, capable, and belong—we can build confidence and overcome these pervasive feelings of fraudulence.
