Spiciness isn't actually a taste like sweet or salty. It's a sensation of pain and heat. Compounds in spicy foods, like capsaicin in chili peppers, activate sensory neurons called polymodal nociceptors. These are the same receptors in your mouth and nose that detect extreme heat. So, when you eat a spicy pepper, your brain is tricked into thinking your mouth is literally burning. This is the opposite of the effect of menthol, which activates your body's cold receptors, creating a cooling sensation.

When these heat receptors are triggered, your body initiates a 'fight or flight' response, as if it's in contact with a real danger. This is why you might start sweating and your heart rate might increase. However, not all spicy foods feel the same. The difference lies in the molecules. Chili peppers and black pepper contain larger, heavier molecules called alkylamides (like capsaicin) that stay in your mouth. In contrast, wasabi, horseradish, and mustard contain smaller, lighter molecules called isothiocyanates, which easily float up into your sinuses, explaining why they burn your nose.

The spiciness of a food is measured on the Scoville scale, which quantifies its capsaicin content based on how much it needs to be diluted before the heat is undetectable. A bell pepper scores 0 Scoville Heat Units (SHU), while ultra-hot peppers like the Carolina Reaper can exceed 2 million SHU, which is about half the intensity of pepper spray. Historically, humans have used spices for at least 23,000 years. One theory suggests we began adding spices to food, especially in warmer climates, to kill off bacteria and preserve it.

Why we continue to eat painfully spicy food remains a bit of a mystery. For many, it's a form of thrill-seeking, similar to riding a roller coaster; they enjoy the adrenaline rush that follows the initial unpleasant sensation. Some studies even link a preference for spicy food to a higher likelihood of enjoying other adrenaline-rich activities. Interestingly, people who love spicy food don't experience the pain any less than those who don't. They've simply grown tougher and learned to enjoy the burn more.
