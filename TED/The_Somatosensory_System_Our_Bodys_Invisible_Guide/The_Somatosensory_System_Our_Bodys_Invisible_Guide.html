The story of Ian Waterman highlights the profound importance of a system we often take for granted. In 1971, after a severe illness, Waterman was diagnosed with a rare form of deafferentation, a neurological condition that left him unable to feel anything from the neck down. His muscles were healthy, but without the constant sensory feedback on his body's position in space, he couldn't sit, stand, or walk. Over time, he ingeniously taught himself to move by using his sight to consciously judge the position of his limbs, regaining control as long as he could see his body.

Waterman's experience reveals the critical role of the somatosensory system, a network that governs all sensations from our body's surface and interior, including touch, pain, temperature, and proprioception—our awareness of our body in space. This system relies on millions of receptor cells in our skin, muscles, and tendons. These include mechanoreceptors for pressure and vibration, thermoreceptors for temperature changes, nociceptors for pain, and proprioceptors deep in our muscles that relay information about our body's position.

These receptors work by sending electrical signals to the brain through attached nerve fibers. The speed of these signals depends on the fiber's thickness. For instance, sharp, immediate pain is transmitted by thicker, myelinated fibers, while the subsequent dull ache travels along thinner, unmyelinated fibers. This is why rubbing an injury can provide temporary relief—the signals for touch travel on much thicker, faster fibers, effectively outpacing the pain signals.

Disruption to this intricate network, whether through nerve damage or autoimmune reactions like in Waterman's case, can have wide-ranging consequences. Losing tactile sensations makes it hard to gauge pressure, while a lack of pain or temperature signals means we might not react to harmful stimuli. Furthermore, the deprivation of social touch can lead to a condition called 'touch starvation,' characterized by anxiety, depression, and even a weakened immune system. These invisible sensations are undeniably vital to how we navigate and interact with the world.
